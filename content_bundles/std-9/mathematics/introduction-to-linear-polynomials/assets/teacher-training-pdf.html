Teaching this chapter

Introduction to Linear Polynomials · Standard 9 Mathematics

The chapter opens with Raju at a shop: red boxes hold 4 pens each, blue boxes hold 5 pencils each, and his total comes to 4x + 5y + 3. Open your own lesson the same way — with a counting story, not a definition on the board.

From there the chapter climbs one idea at a time: algebraic expressions narrow to polynomials in a single variable; polynomials of degree 1 are singled out as linear; linear patterns in tables and tile diagrams reveal a constant difference; that constant difference becomes "growth" or "decay" depending on its sign; growth and decay are unified as y = ax + b; a and b are separated into slope and y-intercept; and the chapter closes by graphing that equation and comparing two lines to see when they run parallel. Every one of the twelve concepts below is a station on this one route — teach the route, not twelve isolated topics.

Slide 2

From expressions to polynomials

Algebraic Expressions. In 4x + 5y + 3, each piece separated by a + or − sign is a term; x and y are variables standing for quantities that can change; 4 and 5 are coefficients, the numbers multiplying those variables; 3 is the constant, the one piece with no variable attached. Have students label Raju's own expression, term by term, before you introduce any other example.

Where this shows up

Buying multiple packs of different items — pens from red boxes, pencils from blue boxes — and writing the total as one expression is exactly this skill, used the moment a shopping list has more than one kind of item.

Univariate Polynomials. A univariate polynomial uses exactly one variable throughout. A taxi fare of a fixed flag-down charge plus a per-kilometre rate is univariate in distance; Raju's 4x + 5y + 3 is not univariate, because it carries both x and y at once.

Where this shows up

Any single-driver cost model — a taxi fare, a water bill by litres used, a print shop charging by page count — is univariate: one changing quantity drives the whole cost.

Degree of a Polynomial. Degree is the highest exponent on the variable, never the number of terms — a constant like 8 still has degree 0, since 8 = 8x⁰. Degree is also a forecast of behaviour: degree 1 means a constant rate of change (steady speed), degree 2 introduces acceleration.

Misconception 1 · Univariate Polynomials

What students think: "4x + 5y + 3 is a univariate polynomial because it has letters in it."
Why: they overgeneralise — any expression with variables looks like it qualifies.
Fix: a univariate polynomial contains exactly one variable; 4x + 5y + 3 is multivariate, not univariate.

Misconception 2 · Degree of a Polynomial

What students think: "3x² + 2x + 1 has degree 3, because it has three terms."
Why: they confuse the word "degree" with a term count.
Fix: degree is the highest exponent present — here, 2 — regardless of how many terms exist.

Try this

Is 7y³ − 2y + 9 univariate? What is its degree, and how many terms does it have — and are those two numbers the same thing?

Slide 3

What makes a polynomial linear

A linear polynomial has degree exactly 1 — the form ax + b, with a ≠ 0. A square garden fence of side length x needs a perimeter of 4x; a chess-club membership costing a flat ₹200 plus ₹50 for every month m is 50m + 200. Both of these are linear polynomials; the fence example simply has no constant term.

Where this shows up

Mobile phone plans — a fixed monthly rental plus a per-minute call charge — are the chapter's own running example of a linear polynomial in disguise.

Misconception 1

What students think: any expression with a variable — including x² or 2/x — is "linear" simply because a letter is present.
Why: overgeneralising the word "polynomial" without checking degree or exponent type.
Fix: a linear polynomial needs degree exactly 1 and only non-negative integer exponents; x² (degree 2) and 2/x (negative exponent) both fail.

Misconception 2

What students think: every linear polynomial must have both an x-term and a constant term, i.e. always look like ax + b with b ≠ 0.
Why: most textbook examples happen to include a constant, so students assume it's required.
Fix: 4x alone, with no constant, is still linear — it is simply the case b = 0.

Classroom activity

A bookstore membership costs a one-time ₹50 joining fee plus ₹20 per book bought. Have students write the linear polynomial for b books bought, then verify against the two facts above: is it degree 1? Does it need a constant term to still count as linear?

Try this

Is 4x, on its own, a valid linear polynomial? Justify using the degree-1 rule, not intuition.

Slide 4

Finding the pattern, before naming it

The chapter's own growing-tile pattern gives 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 tiles at stages 1 through 5. The difference between consecutive stages is always 2, so the pattern is linear, and its nth term is 2n − 1 — check: stage 1 gives 2(1) − 1 = 1; stage 5 gives 2(5) − 1 = 9. A linear pattern is defined by that constant difference, never merely by "the numbers keep increasing."

Where this shows up

Pocket money of ₹100 spent down by ₹5 a day leaves 100 − 5n rupees after n days — a linear pattern running downward instead of up.

Misconception 1

What students think: 2, 4, 7, 11 is a linear pattern because the numbers "keep adding."
Why: they check that the pattern is additive, but not that the amount added stays the same — here the differences are 2, 3, 4.
Fix: a linear pattern needs a constant difference, checked at every step, not just an increasing trend.

Misconception 2

What students think: for the tile pattern, the nth term is n + 1.
Why: they confuse the term's position (n) with the amount it changes by each step; n + 1 would only add 1 each time, giving 2, 3, 4, 5 — not 1, 3, 5, 7.
Fix: the coefficient of n carries the constant difference (here, 2); the correct term is 2n − 1.

Try this

Using the tile pattern's nth term 2n − 1, how many tiles are in stage 20? How would you check your answer against the stage-by-stage count?

Slide 5

Growth and decay, side by side

Linear Growth. The chapter's cost function C(d) = 100 + 60d — a base fare of ₹100 plus ₹60 per kilometre — grows by the exact same amount, 60, every additional kilometre. That constant additive step, never a multiplying factor, is what makes it linear growth rather than, say, exponential growth.

Linear Decay. A water tank's height h(t) = 3 − 0.5t falls by the same 0.5 metres every month: at t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, h gives 3, 2.5, 2, 1.5, 1. Decay mirrors growth exactly — constant subtraction over equal time intervals.

Where this shows up

Growth: taxi fares rising with distance travelled. Decay: the balance remaining on a prepaid recharge card after each call, dropping by a fixed amount per use.

Misconception 1 · Linear Growth

What students think: linear growth means values double, or multiply by a constant factor, each step.
Why: carried over from earlier work with multiplication tables and repeated doubling.
Fix: linear growth adds a constant amount repeatedly; it never multiplies.

Misconception 2 · Linear Decay

What students think: any line with a negative slope represents "decay."
Why: focusing only on the sign of the slope, ignoring whether the real-world variable is actually a diminishing quantity over time.
Fix: decay needs a quantity that steadily decreases over equal intervals, typically of time — a falling profit-per-item graph as price rises is a negative slope, but not decay.

Try this

Using h(t) = 3 − 0.5t, what is the tank's height after 5 months — and is this an example of growth or decay? How do you know?

Slide 6

The general linear relationship, y = ax + b

A telecom plan charging ₹200 a month plus ₹10 per GB gives total cost y = 10x + 200: a = 10 is the rate per unit (the slope), b = 200 is the fixed starting amount (the y-intercept). Every growth, decay, and pattern story earlier in this chapter is this same form — only the specific values and signs of a and b change from story to story.

Where this shows up

Any "fixed charge plus rate" billing — electricity, broadband data, a gym membership with a joining fee and a monthly rate — fits y = ax + b directly.

Misconception 1

What students think: every linear relationship must pass through the origin, so y is always directly proportional to x.
Why: carried over from direct-variation work in earlier grades, where y = ax (no constant) was the only form seen.
Fix: y = 2x − 1 is linear and does not pass through the origin; b need not be zero.

Misconception 2

What students think: slope only describes how steep a line looks, with no quantitative meaning.
Why: a purely visual reading of the graph, with no link back to the ratio of change in y to change in x.
Fix: slope a is the exact amount y changes for every unit increase in x — in y = 2x − 1, every unit increase in x adds exactly 2 to y.

Classroom activity

Give students a table of a telecom plan's total cost at 0, 5, 10 and 15 GB used. Have them work out a and b for themselves, from the numbers alone, before you write y = ax + b on the board.

Try this

For y = 10x + 200, what does a = 10 mean in the phone-plan story, and what does b = 200 mean? Which one would change if the per-GB rate changed?

Slide 7

Slope: steepness relative to y = x

For lines of the form y = ax, the coefficient a sets the steepness relative to the reference line y = x itself: y = 2x is steeper than y = x, while y = 0.5x is shallower — all three still pass through the same origin. A steeper ramp, with a larger a, climbs further for the same horizontal run, which is exactly why a steep ramp is harder to push a loaded cart up.

Where this shows up

A wheelchair ramp's slope determines how much effort is needed — building codes set a maximum allowed slope for exactly this reason.

Misconception 1

What students think: in y = ax, the coefficient a is the y-intercept.
Why: since y = ax does pass through (0, 0), students sometimes conflate the coefficient itself with "where the line starts."
Fix: a controls steepness, not position; the y-intercept here is 0 because there is no separate b term, and they are two different numbers in general.

Misconception 2

What students think: a slope of −5 is steeper than a slope of 2, simply because 5 is bigger than 2.
Why: comparing magnitude without considering what the sign means for direction.
Fix: steepness compares the absolute value of the slope; −5 is indeed steeper than 2 in magnitude, but the sign separately tells you the line falls rather than rises — both facts matter, for different questions.

Try this

Which of y = 3x and y = −5x is steeper? Which one rises and which one falls? Are these the same question or two different ones?

Slide 8

Y-intercept: where the line starts

Setting x = 0 in y = ax + b always gives y = b — the single point (0, b) where the line crosses the y-axis. A phone plan charging a fixed ₹200 a month plus a per-minute rate has y-intercept 200: that is exactly the cost before a single minute of calling is used, the "starting cost" of the plan.

Where this shows up

Any "starting value before anything happens" — a taxi's base fare before distance is added, a savings account's opening balance before interest accrues — is a y-intercept in the real world.

Misconception 1

What students think: the constant b in y = ax + b is where the line crosses the x-axis.
Why: not clearly distinguishing the two different substitutions — setting x = 0 versus setting y = 0.
Fix: b is found by setting x = 0, giving the point (0, b) on the y-axis; the x-intercept is a separate calculation, found by setting y = 0.

Misconception 2

What students think: a y-intercept is always a positive number.
Why: focusing on "distance from the origin" rather than direction.
Fix: y = 3x − 2 has y-intercept −2, a negative value, meaning the line crosses below the origin — the sign of b tells you above or below.

Try this

A line passes through (0, 4) and (2, 10). What is its y-intercept, and how do you know without plotting a single extra point?

Slide 9

Graphing a line, and lines that never meet

Any two points fix a straight line completely. For y = 2x + 1, plot A (0, 1) using the y-intercept, then plot B (3, 7) by choosing any other x, and draw a straight edge through both — this two-point method is exactly how an auto-rickshaw fare of ₹25 for the first 2 km plus ₹15 per extra kilometre gets graphed.

Misconception 1 · Graphing Linear Equations

What students think: every straight line must pass through the origin.
Why: overgeneralising from y = ax examples, which always pass through (0, 0).
Fix: only lines with b = 0 pass through the origin; y = ax + b with b ≠ 0 crosses the y-axis at (0, b) instead.

Railway tracks are the clearest real-world model: y = 3x + 1 and y = 3x − 4 share the same slope, 3, but cross the y-axis at different points, 1 and −4. Equal slope with different intercepts is exactly what keeps the two lines running side by side forever, never meeting.

Where this shows up

Railway tracks, the two edges of a straight road, and the rungs of a ladder are all everyday parallel lines — equal slope, different position.

Misconception 2 · Parallel Lines

What students think: any two lines that look like they don't cross, by eye, are parallel; and separately, that equal slopes alone are enough to guarantee two distinct parallel lines.
Why: relying on visual inspection instead of comparing slopes algebraically, and overlooking the role of the intercept.
Fix: parallelism must be checked by comparing slopes exactly; and equal slope with an equal intercept too gives one coincident line, not two parallel ones — parallel requires equal slope and different intercepts.

Try this

Are y = 3x + 2 and y = 3x + 2 parallel, coincident, or neither? What about y = 3x + 2 and y = 3x − 4? What is the one number you check to tell these two cases apart?

Before you close the chapter

1Expressions, univariate, degree: three separate questions — how many variables, what's the highest exponent, and neither is the term count.
2Linear polynomial (ax + b, degree 1, non-negative integer exponents only) underlies every pattern, growth and decay story in this chapter — keep returning students to that one form.
3Linear pattern, growth, decay and relationship are all the same constant-difference idea, told four different ways — show students the thread, don't teach them as unrelated topics.
4Slope a and intercept b answer different questions: a is steepness and rate of change; b is where the line starts, and it can be negative.
5Two points are always enough to graph a line — the two-point method is the one procedure that carries through every example in this chapter.
6Parallel needs equal slope AND different intercept — equal slope with equal intercept gives one coincident line, not two parallel ones.
