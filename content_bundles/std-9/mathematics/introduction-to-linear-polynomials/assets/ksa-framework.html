Section 1

Overview

Twelve concepts in one continuous climb: algebraic expressions narrow to univariate polynomials, which are classified by degree, and degree 1 is singled out as linear. Linear patterns, growth and decay are three faces of the same constant-difference idea, which is then formalised as y = ax + b and split into slope and y-intercept, before being graphed and compared for parallelism. Every row below traces to a specific line in the chapter – Raju's 4x + 5y + 3, the growing tile pattern 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, the cost function C(d) = 100 + 60d, the telecom bill y = 10x + 200 – and every strategy is one the chapter's own concept data recommends, not a generic suggestion.

Section 3

Linear Polynomial

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
A linear polynomial has degree exactly 1: the form ax + b, a ≠ 0Test whether a given expression is linearProcedural fluencyBasic
A square fence of side x has perimeter 4x – a linear polynomial with no constant termModel a real quantity as a linear polynomialMathematical modellingIntermediate
A linear polynomial need not have both a variable term and a constant termRecognise 4x, with b = 0, as still linearConceptual reasoningIntermediate
Recommended strategy – inquiry-based teachingHave students calculate the perimeter for several side lengths and discover the constant rate of change themselves, before the term "linear" is introduced.
Where this shows upMobile phone plans with a fixed monthly rental plus a per-minute rate, and bookstore memberships with a joining fee plus a per-book charge, are both linear polynomials in disguise.

Linear Pattern

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
A linear pattern has a constant difference between every consecutive termCheck a sequence for a constant differenceVerification reasoningBasic
The tile pattern 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 has nth term 2n − 1Derive the nth term from a visual or tabular patternInductive reasoningIntermediate

Where this shows upPocket money of ₹100 reduced by ₹5 a day, left as 100 − 5n rupees after n days, is the same linear-pattern reasoning running in reverse.
Section 5

Linear Growth and Linear Decay

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
Growth: a quantity increases by the same fixed amount each step – C(d) = 100 + 60dRecognise constant additive growth in a table of valuesPattern recognitionBasic
Decay: a quantity decreases by the same fixed amount each step – h(t) = 3 − 0.5tRecognise constant additive decay in a table of valuesPattern recognitionBasic
Growth adds; exponential change multiplies – the two are easily confusedDistinguish linear growth from exponential growthDiscriminationAdvanced
Decay requires a genuinely diminishing quantity over equal time steps, not merely a negative slopeJudge whether a falling line truly represents decayContextual reasoningAdvanced
Recommended strategy – activity-based teachingBuild both tables of values side by side on the board, then both graphs, so the mirrored constant-step structure of growth and decay is visible at a glance.
Where this shows upA taxi fare rising with distance (growth); a prepaid recharge balance falling by a fixed amount per call (decay).

Linear Relationship, y = ax + b

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
y = ax + b unifies pattern, growth and decay into one algebraic formWrite a real scenario in the form y = ax + bMathematical modellingIntermediate
a is the rate of change; b is the fixed starting amountIdentify a and b from a table or a word problemInterpretationIntermediate

Section 7

Slope of a Line

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
In y = ax, a sets the steepness relative to the reference line y = xCompare the steepness of two lines from their slopesComparative reasoningBasic
A larger |a| means a steeper line; the sign of a sets rising versus fallingSeparate magnitude (steepness) from sign (direction)DiscriminationIntermediate
Slope is a rate: the exact amount y changes for every unit increase in xState slope as a rate of change, not just a visual impressionQuantitative reasoningAdvanced
Recommended strategy – activity-based teachingHands-on graphing of several y = ax lines on the same axes lets students see steepness directly, rather than only compute it.
Where this shows upA wheelchair ramp's slope, capped by building codes, determines exactly how much effort is needed to climb it.
Section 8

Y-Intercept

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
Setting x = 0 in y = ax + b gives the point (0, b), the y-interceptFind the y-intercept from an equation directlyProcedural fluencyBasic
The y-intercept can be negative, zero, or positiveRead the sign of b to tell where the line crossesInterpretationIntermediate
Two points on a line are enough to determine its y-intercept without plottingWork backwards from two points to the y-interceptAlgebraic reasoningAdvanced
Recommended strategy – activity-based teachingGraphing several lines with varying y-intercepts side by side makes the vertical shift, and the meaning of b, visible and kinesthetic rather than purely symbolic.
Where this shows upA phone plan's fixed monthly charge, and a taxi's base fare before distance is added, are both real-world y-intercepts – the cost before anything else happens.
Section 9

Graphing Linear Equations and Parallel Lines

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
Two points fix a straight line completely – the two-point methodGraph a linear equation from two plotted pointsProcedural fluencyBasic
Equal slope with different y-intercepts gives two parallel lines that never meetTest two equations for parallelism by comparing slopesAnalytical thinkingIntermediate
Equal slope with an equal intercept gives one coincident line, not two parallel onesDistinguish parallel from coincident linesDiscriminationAdvanced
Recommended strategy – activity-based teachingStudents physically plot several lines with the same slope but different intercepts on graph paper, visually confirming they never meet, which solidifies the abstract rule far more than a statement of it does.
Where this shows upAn auto-rickshaw fare graphed from its base charge and per-km rate; railway tracks, the two edges of a straight road, and ladder rungs as everyday parallel lines.
