Introduction to Linear Polynomials

The chapter opens with Raju at a shop: red boxes hold 4 pens each, blue boxes hold 5 pencils each, and his total is 4x + 5y + 3. Open your lesson the same way — a counting story, not a definition. The whole chapter is one long escalation from this expression to a graphed line, so keep returning to concrete contexts (fences, phone plans, taxi fares) before naming each new term.

Slide 2

From expressions to polynomials

In 4x + 5y + 3, each piece separated by + or − is a term; x, y are variables; 4, 5 are coefficients; 3 is the constant. Label Raju's own expression first.

A univariate polynomial uses exactly one variable — a taxi fare of flag-down fee plus rate × km is univariate; Raju's 4x + 5y + 3 is not.

Degree is the highest exponent, not the term count — a constant like 8 still has degree 0 (8 = 8x⁰). Degree also forecasts: degree 1 is constant speed, degree 2 is acceleration.

Common misconception

Two errors chain together: students call 4x + 5y + 3 univariate because "it has letters," and call 3x² + 2x + 1 degree 3 for its three terms. Correct both the same way — count variables for univariate, count the highest exponent for degree.

Is 7y³ − 2y + 9 univariate? What is its degree?

Slide 3

What makes a polynomial linear

A linear polynomial has degree exactly 1 — the form ax + b. A square garden fence of side x needs perimeter 4x; a chess-club membership of ₹200 plus ₹50 per month is 50m + 200. Both are linear; the second just happens to carry a constant term too.

Common misconception

Students assume every linear polynomial needs both an x-term and a constant, so 4x alone looks "incomplete" to them, and conversely x² or 2/x look "linear" because they contain a variable. Anchor the test on two facts only: degree 1, and only non-negative integer exponents.

Try this

A bookstore membership costs a one-time ₹50 fee plus ₹20 per book. Have students write the linear polynomial for b books, then check: is it degree 1? Does it need a constant term to qualify?

Try this

Is 4x, on its own, a valid linear polynomial? Why?

Slide 4

Finding the pattern, before naming it

The chapter's own growing-tile pattern gives 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 tiles at stages 1–5 — a constant difference of 2 every stage, so the nth term is 2n − 1. A linear pattern is defined by that constant difference, not merely by "the numbers keep going up."

Common misconception

Two traps: students accept 2, 4, 7, 11 as linear because it "keeps adding," without checking the difference is constant (2, 3, 4 — it isn't); and they write the nth term as n + 1 instead of 2n − 1, missing that the coefficient of n carries the constant difference, not just the position count.

Try this

Using the tile pattern's nth term 2n − 1, how many tiles are in stage 20?

Slide 5

Growth and decay, side by side

The chapter's cost function C(d) = 100 + 60d — a base fare plus ₹60 per km — grows by the same fixed amount, 60, every extra kilometre. That constant additive step, not any multiplying, is what makes it linear growth.

A tank's height h(t) = 3 − 0.5t falls by the same 0.5 m every month: t = 0,1,2,3,4 gives h = 3, 2.5, 2, 1.5, 1. Decay is the mirror of growth — constant subtraction over equal intervals, usually of time.

Common misconception

Students conflate linear decay with exponential decay (constant subtraction vs. constant halving), and separately assume any falling line is "decay" even when the variable isn't time — profit-per-item falling as price rises is a negative slope, not decay. Decay needs a diminishing quantity over equal time steps.

Try this

Using h(t) = 3 − 0.5t, what is the tank's height after 5 months — and is that growth or decay?

Slide 6

The general linear relationship, y = ax + b

A telecom plan charging ₹200 a month plus ₹10 per GB gives total cost y = 10x + 200: here a = 10 is the rate per unit, b = 200 is the fixed part. Growth, decay and patterns are all this same form — only the signs and values of a and b change.

Common misconception

From earlier direct-variation work, students expect every linear relationship to pass through the origin — but y = 2x − 1 does not. A second confusion: treating slope as "just how steep it looks" rather than as the exact amount y changes for every unit increase in x.

Try this

Give students a table of a telecom plan's cost at 0, 5, 10 and 15 GB. Have them find a and b themselves before you write y = ax + b on the board.

Try this

For y = 10x + 200, what does a = 10 represent in the phone-plan story, and what does b = 200 represent?

Slide 7

Slope: steepness relative to y = x

For y = ax, a sets the steepness relative to y = x itself: y = 2x is steeper, y = 0.5x is shallower, both through the same origin. A steeper ramp (larger a) takes less run for the same rise, which is why it's harder to push a cart up.

Common misconception

Students sometimes call a "the y-intercept" since in y = ax the line does pass through (0, 0) — but a controls steepness, not position; b (absent here) would be the intercept. A second trap: assuming slope −5 is steeper than slope 2 purely because 5 > 2, ignoring that direction and magnitude both matter.

Try this

Which of y = 3x and y = −5x is steeper, and how do you justify that using magnitude, not sign?

Slide 8

Y-intercept: where the line starts

Setting x = 0 in y = ax + b always gives y = b — the point (0, b) where the line crosses the y-axis. A ₹200-a-month phone plan with a per-minute charge has y-intercept 200: that's the cost before a single minute is used.

Common misconception

Students swap the y-intercept (set x = 0) with the x-intercept (set y = 0), and often assume b must be positive — missing lines like y = 3x − 2, whose y-intercept is the negative value −2, meaning the line crosses below the origin.

Try this

A line passes through (0, 4) and (2, 10). What is its y-intercept, and how do you know without plotting?

Slide 9

Graphing a line, and lines that never meet

Any two points fix a line. For y = 2x + 1, plot A (0, 1) from the intercept and B (3, 7) from a chosen x, then draw straight through both — the same method behind graphing an auto-rickshaw fare.

Railway tracks model it: y = 3x + 1 and y = 3x − 4 share slope 3 but cross the y-axis at different points, so they run side by side, never meeting.

Common misconception

Students judge "no obvious crossing" by eye — parallelism must be checked by comparing slopes. Sharper trap: equal slope AND equal intercept is one coincident line, not two parallel ones; parallel needs equal slope, different intercepts.

Try this

Are y = 3x + 2 and y = 3x + 2 parallel, coincident, or neither? What about y = 3x + 2 and y = 3x − 4?
