Orienting Yourself: The Use of Coordinates

The chapter opens with Reiaan and Shalini, not with axes: Shalini guides her brother Reiaan around their new room using pins and thread on a floor grid. Open your own lesson the same way before you name an x-axis or a y-axis. The chapter's 1.1 also carries Bharat's own history of grid-based thinking, from Sindhu-Sarasvati street grids to Baudhayana and Brahmagupta's zero: use it as your hook, not a skippable aside.

Slide 2

The Cartesian coordinate system

Two perpendicular lines, the x-axis and y-axis, meet at the origin O (0, 0); distances right or up are positive, left or down are negative. This is abstract until it is physical: use a large floor grid or graph-paper plotting, and let students place pins before you name a single term.

Try this

Ask: which two facts fully fix a point's location? (Its distance from each axis, and the direction — positive or negative.)

Slide 3

Misconception: which number comes first

Common misconception

Students often write (y, x), swapping the order. The cause is rarely carelessness — it is that they have not fixed "horizontal first" as a rule. Say it the same way every time: x is sideways, y is up-down, and the point is always (sideways, up-down).

Try this

Cold-call: "A point is 3 left, 2 up from O. Say its coordinates aloud before writing them."

Slide 4

The origin

The origin O (0, 0) is where the axes cross, and it is the one point in the lesson that never moves. A surprising number of students think the origin shifts with them as they walk a floor grid — correct this the first time you see it, not after it has become a habit.

Try this

Ask a student to stand anywhere on the floor grid, then ask the class: "Has the origin moved?"

Slide 5

Points that sit on an axis

A point on the x-axis has the form (x, 0); a point on the y-axis has the form (0, y) — the textbook's own examples are B (4.5, 0) and H (0, 4). The sign of the non-zero number still tells direction: positive x is right of O, positive y is above O.

Try this

Give G (0, −4.5) from the chapter. Ask: which axis, and which direction from O?

Slide 6

What (x, y) actually measures

x is the perpendicular distance from the y-axis, measured along the x-axis; y is the perpendicular distance from the x-axis, measured along the y-axis. Students confuse this because "x-coordinate" sounds like "distance from the x-axis" — say explicitly that it is the opposite.

Try this

For Q (−5, 3): which distance is measured along the x-axis, and which along the y-axis?

Slide 7

The four quadrants

The axes cut the plane into four quadrants, numbered I to IV counter-clockwise from the top right. Quadrant I is (+, +), II is (−, +), III is (−, −), IV is (+, −). Teach the counter-clockwise order explicitly — students default to clockwise because that is how a clock reads.

Try this

Which quadrant holds a point with a negative x-coordinate and a negative y-coordinate?

Slide 8

Misconception: axes are not inside a quadrant

Common misconception

This is the chapter's most repeated error: students assign (0, 5) or (3, 0) to a quadrant. A quadrant needs both coordinates non-zero — a point on an axis is a boundary, not an interior point, and belongs to no quadrant at all.

Try this

A student says "(0, 7) is in Quadrant I because both numbers are positive or zero." Where is the error?

Slide 9

Reading the signs quickly

Once sign conventions are secure, speed matters more than re-deriving them each time. A quadrant-identification game — call out a point, students physically step to the matching quadrant on the floor grid — turns slow reasoning into fast, confident recall.

Try this

Run a floor-grid "quadrant race": call out coordinates; students race to the right quadrant and justify the sign pattern.

Try this

Call (−3, −2). Which quadrant, and how fast can the class answer without counting on fingers?

Slide 10

Distance between two points

Build the formula from the chapter's own triangle ADM, with A (3, 4) and D (7, 1): the horizontal leg AM = 4, the vertical leg MD = 3, and the Baudhayana-Pythagoras theorem gives AD = √(4² + 3²) = 5. Let students derive this before you generalise it to √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²).

Try this

Ask students to find MA using the same figure, before you reveal that DM and MA together with AD complete the triangle's three sides.

Slide 11

Misconception: skipping the square

Common misconception

Students write the distance as (x₂−x₁) + (y₂−y₁), dropping the squares and the root. This usually means the formula was memorised, not derived from the right triangle — go back to triangle ADM and the Baudhayana-Pythagoras theorem rather than re-stating the formula louder.

Try this

A student claims distance is always non-negative "because the question says so." Ask them to explain it instead using the square.

Slide 12

Reflection in the y-axis

Reflecting (x, y) in the y-axis gives (−x, y): only the x-coordinate changes sign. The chapter reflects triangle ADM itself, so A (3, 4) and D (7, 1) become A′ (−3, 4) and D′ (−7, 1) — and AD still equals A′D′ = 5, because reflection preserves length.

Try this

If reflection were in the x-axis instead, which coordinate would change sign this time?

Slide 13

Misconception: which coordinate flips

Common misconception

Because the y-axis is vertical, students expect the y-coordinate to flip. It is the opposite: reflecting in the y-axis changes x, because x measures distance from that very axis. Anchor the rule to "the coordinate measured from the mirror line is the one that flips."

Try this

A student reflects (3, 4) in the y-axis and gets (3, −4). Where exactly did the reasoning go wrong?

Slide 14

Finding a midpoint, by discovery

The textbook never states the midpoint formula outright — it builds it through the S/M/T table in Exercise Set 1.2 and then the question "if M (−7, 1) is the midpoint of A (3, −4) and B (x, y), find B." Run those two exercises as written before you name the formula ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2).

Try this

Hand out the chapter's S/M/T table: students decide, row by row, whether M is the midpoint of ST — before any formula is on the board.

Try this

For S (−3, 0) and T (3, 0), is M (0, 0) the midpoint? How do you know?
