Orienting Yourself: The Use of Coordinates

The chapter opens with Reiaan and Shalini, not with axes: Shalini guides her brother Reiaan around their new room using pins and thread on a floor grid. Open your lesson the same way before naming an x-axis or y-axis. The chapter's own history — Sindhu-Sarasvati street grids, Baudhayana, Brahmagupta's zero — is your hook, not a skippable aside.

Slide 2

The coordinate system

Two perpendicular lines, the x-axis and y-axis, meet at the origin O (0, 0) — the one point that never moves. Right or up is positive; left or down, negative. Teach it physically first, before naming a single term.

Students often think the origin shifts with them as they walk a floor grid — correct this the first time you see it.

Common misconception

Students write (y, x). Say it the same way every time: x is sideways, y is up-down.

Try this

Ask: which two facts fix a point's location? (Distance from each axis, and direction.)

Slide 3

Points that sit on an axis

A point on the x-axis has the form (x, 0); on the y-axis, (0, y) — the textbook's own examples are B (4.5, 0) and H (0, 4). The sign of the non-zero number still tells direction: positive x is right of O, positive y is above O.

Try this

Give G (0, −4.5) from the chapter. Which axis, and which direction from O?

Slide 4

Coordinates and quadrants

x is the perpendicular distance from the y-axis; y is the perpendicular distance from the x-axis. Students often swap this — say explicitly which measures which.

The axes cut the plane into four quadrants, I to IV counter-clockwise from the top right. QI is (+,+), QII (−,+), QIII (−,−), QIV (+,−). Students default to clockwise, like a clock — teach the order explicitly.

Try this

Which quadrant holds a point with a negative x-coordinate and a negative y-coordinate?

Slide 5

Reading the signs quickly

Once signs are secure, a quadrant-identification game turns slow reasoning into fast, confident recall.

Common misconception

This is the chapter's most repeated error: students assign (0, 5) or (3, 0) to a quadrant. A quadrant needs both coordinates non-zero — an axis point is a boundary, not an interior point.

Try this

Run a floor-grid "quadrant race": call out coordinates; students race to the right quadrant and justify the sign pattern.

Try this

A student says "(0, 7) is in Quadrant I." Where is the error?

Slide 6

Distance between two points

Build the formula from triangle ADM: A (3, 4), D (7, 1). Horizontal leg AM = 4, vertical leg MD = 3, and the Baudhayana-Pythagoras theorem gives AD = √(4² + 3²) = 5. Let students derive this before generalising it to √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²).

Common misconception

Students write (x₂−x₁) + (y₂−y₁), dropping the squares and root — the formula was memorised, not derived. Return to triangle ADM rather than restating it louder.

Try this

Ask students to find MA using the same figure.

Slide 7

Reflection in the y-axis

Reflecting (x, y) in the y-axis gives (−x, y): only x changes sign. A (3, 4) and D (7, 1) become A′ (−3, 4) and D′ (−7, 1) — and AD still equals A′D′ = 5.

Common misconception

Because the y-axis is vertical, students expect y to flip. It's the opposite — x measures distance from that axis, so x flips.

Try this

A student reflects (3, 4) and gets (3, −4). Where did the reasoning go wrong?

Slide 8

Finding a midpoint, by discovery

The textbook never states the midpoint formula — it's built through the S/M/T table and "if M (−7, 1) is the midpoint of A (3, −4) and B (x, y), find B." Run these before naming ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2).

Try this

Hand out the S/M/T table: students decide, row by row, whether M is the midpoint of ST — before any formula is on the board.

Try this

For S (−3, 0) and T (3, 0), is M (0, 0) the midpoint? How do you know?
