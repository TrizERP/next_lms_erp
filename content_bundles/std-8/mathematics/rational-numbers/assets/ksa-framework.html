Section 1

Overview

Twenty-two concepts organised around one question, asked of four operations across four number systems: does this property hold? Every row below traces to a specific line in the chapter – the equation chain x + 2 = 13 through 2x = 3, the closure tables for whole numbers and integers, and the worked examples for associativity and distributivity – and every strategy is one the chapter's own concept data recommends.

Section 2

Why Rational Numbers

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
Naturals, whole numbers, integers and rationals each exist because an equation demanded themMatch an equation (x+2=13, x+5=5, x+18=5, 2x=3) to the number system it requiresAbstract-to-concrete translationBasic
A rational number has the form p/q, p and q integers, q ≠ 0Write an integer such as −2 or 4 in p/q formClassificationBasic
Properties are testable claims, not a list to memoriseTest a property on a specific pair before generalising itEmpirical reasoningIntermediate
Recommended strategy – activity-based teachingSolve the chapter's own equation chain on the board, pausing at each failure to ask "what number would work?" before naming the next number system.
Where this shows upA winter temperature of −13° is read straight off a thermometer; a machinist cutting stock to three halves of a unit length works from the exact ratio 3/2, not a rounded one.
Section 3

Closure

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
Whole numbers: closed under +, ×; not −, ÷. Integers add − to the closed listFind a counterexample that disproves closure for a given system and operationCritical thinkingIntermediate
Rationals are closed under +, −, ×, and ÷ except by zeroVerify closure by computing a specific sum, difference, product or quotientProcedural fluencyBasic
One failing pair settles closure for the whole set, regardless of how many pairs succeedDistinguish "closure holds for most pairs" from "closure holds"Logical reasoningAdvanced
Recommended strategy – inquiry-based teachingHand out pairs of numbers from each system and have students test each operation themselves, filling in a Yes/No grid, before stating the general rule.
Where this shows upA spreadsheet returns a division error when a denominator cell holds zero; integer variables in programming truncate on division precisely because it can leave the set.
Section 4

Commutativity

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
Addition and multiplication are commutative for rationals; subtraction and division are notReorder a rational expression and predict whether the value changesPattern recognitionBasic
A counterexample for integers (5−7 ≠ 7−5) still disproves commutativity for rationalsExplain why a failure in a smaller system carries to a larger one that contains itLogical transferAdvanced
Recommended strategy – activity-based teachingHave students physically swap two number cards around an operation symbol and compute both orders, for all four operations, rather than assuming the addition result transfers.
Where this shows upTotalling a shopping bill gives the same amount whatever order the prices are added; taking ₹200 from a ₹500 balance is not the same transaction as the reverse.
Section 5

Associativity

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
Addition and multiplication are associative for rationals; subtraction and division are notCompute both groupings of a three-term expression and compareProcedural fluencyIntermediate
Division does not inherit associativity from multiplication, despite being its inverseExplain why an inverse operation need not share its partner's propertiesConceptual reasoningAdvanced
Recommended strategy – inquiry-based teachingLet students discover that grouping changes the answer for subtraction and division by trying it themselves, before being told which operations are associative.
Where this shows upSplitting a long bill into two part-totals and adding those gives the same amount as one long addition; scaling a recipe by three fractional factors gives the same batch size in any grouping.
Section 6

Zero and One

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
0 is the additive identity; 1 is the multiplicative identity for rational numbersApply the correct identity to simplify a + 0 or a × 1Procedural fluencyBasic
Zero is not the multiplicative identity, and one is not the additive identityDistinguish which identity belongs to which operationDiscriminationIntermediate
Recommended strategy – activity-based teachingHave students fill in a + 0, a × 0, a + 1, a × 1 for the same rational a side by side, so the two genuine identities stand out against the two near-misses.
Where this shows upA day with no spending leaves a household budget's running total unchanged; converting a measurement by a unit ratio equal to one changes the units but never the quantity.
Section 7

Distributivity

KnowledgeSkillAbilityLevel
a(b+c) = ab+ac and a(b−c) = ab−ac for all rational a, b, cExpand a bracketed product using distributivityProcedural fluencyBasic
Distributivity also works in reverse, to gather a common factorUse distributivity, with commutativity and associativity, to simplify a chain of operationsStrategic calculationAdvanced
Recommended strategy – activity-based teachingDraw an arrow from the outside factor to every term inside the bracket before any multiplying begins, every time, until the habit is automatic.
Where this shows upFinding the cost of six items priced as a sum of two parts, by multiplying each part and adding; a shopkeeper totalling a repeated fractional discount once for a whole order.

Section 11

Closing

This chapter is less about twenty-two separate facts than about one repeating test, run eight times: does this property survive for this operation, in this number system? Teach the test, and the facts follow.
