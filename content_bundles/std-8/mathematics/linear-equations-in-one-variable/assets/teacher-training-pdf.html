Teaching this chapter

Linear Equations in One Variable · Standard 8 Mathematics

5x = 25 is an equation; 5x alone is an expression. That one distinction — the equals sign — is the whole chapter's foundation. Open your lesson by sorting a mixed list of expressions and equations before naming either term.

From there the chapter builds one technique at a time: balancing both sides, transposing as balancing's shortcut, handling a variable on both sides, then clearing fractions and opening brackets to reduce a messier equation to the same simple form. Every method ends the same way — check the answer against both original sides.

Slide 2

Expression or equation?

5x, 2x − 3, 3x + y are expressions — no equals sign, nothing to solve. 5x = 25, 2x − 3 = 9 are equations: a claim that two expressions have the same value. Teach the sort before the vocabulary — hand students a mixed list and have them separate the two piles first.

LHS is the expression left of the equals sign, RHS the expression right of it. Show an equation like 9 = 2x − 3 early — the bare number isn't always on the right.

x = 5 solves 2x − 3 = 7 because LHS = 2×5−3 = 7 = RHS. x = 10 does not: LHS = 17 ≠ RHS. Only certain values keep the two sides equal — those are the solutions, not just any value the variable could take.

Where this shows up

A spreadsheet treats a typed formula and a typed comparison test as two different kinds of entry — the expression/equation distinction is built into software, not just textbook vocabulary.

Misconception 1

What students think: an expression like 2x − 3 can be "solved" for x, as though it were an equation waiting for a missing half.
Fix: ask what the expression is supposed to equal — there is nothing to set it against.

Misconception 2

What students think: the RHS always holds the answer and the LHS always holds the working, since arithmetic writes the result after the equals sign.
Fix: show equations like 9 = 2x − 3 where the bare number sits on the left instead.

Slide 3

What makes an equation linear

2xy + 5 has two variables, x and y — it's excluded here. This chapter restricts to equations with exactly one variable, however many times that one variable's letter appears in the expression.

2x, 3y − 7, 12 − 5z are linear: the highest power of the variable is 1. x² + 1 is not. "Linear" describes the exponent, never how many terms an expression has, and never how long the equation looks.

One variable plus degree 1 together define a linear equation in one variable — the type studied through this entire chapter, and the simple equations from earlier classes were always this type.

Where this shows up

A costing model with a fixed charge plus a rate for each unit made is a linear equation in one variable — the unit count is the single unknown.

Misconception 1

What students think: repeated letters count as several variables — the x in 2x and the x in 3x look like two variables.
Fix: circle the distinct letters, not the terms, so that 2x + 3x plainly shows one variable.

Misconception 2

What students think: the 5 in 12 − 5z is read as a power of z, since it sits right beside the letter.
Fix: contrast 5z with z raised to the power five, showing exactly where an index is written.

Slide 4

The balance principle

Treat the two sides of an equation as a balance: perform the same operation on both sides, and the balance holds. x + 3 = 7, subtract 3 from both sides, and x + 3 − 3 = 7 − 3 gives x = 4 — the scale never tips.

Checking means substituting the answer back into the original equation — a different route than the one that found it, not a repeat of the same working. Sharing a bill equally means the same adjustment must be made to every person's share — the same balancing logic, used daily.

Misconception 1

What students think: an operation can be performed on whichever side looks awkward, leaving the other side untouched.
Fix: use a real two-pan balance — lift a weight off only one pan, and the tilt is immediate and obvious.

Misconception 2

What students think: checking an answer means redoing the same solving steps.
Fix: substitute the answer into the original equation instead — a different route than the one the working took, which can catch a repeated mistake the redo would miss.

Slide 5

Transposing: the shortcut for balancing

Subtracting x from both sides amounts to moving x across the equals sign with its sign flipped — that shortcut is transposing. It is not a separate rule to memorise; it is the balancing step written faster. Equation-solving software rearranges terms by exactly this rule when it isolates a variable.

Misconception

What students think: a term changes sign when it "crosses over," recited as a rule with no reason behind it.
Fix: write the full addition or subtraction on both sides once, then show the shortcut produces the identical line — the rule has a derivation, not just a result.

Classroom activity

Give students an equation with four terms to transpose, one at a time. After each transposition, have them verify the new line is correct by redoing that one step as a full balancing move (add/subtract on both sides) rather than trusting the shortcut blindly.

Try this

Transpose the 7 in 7x + 7 = −28 to the other side. What does the equation become?

Slide 6

A variable on each side

2x − 3 = x + 2 has expressions with a variable on both sides, not just a bare number on one. Comparing two phone tariffs, each charging per minute, naturally produces this shape of equation — the unknown minute count sits on both sides of the comparison.

Subtracting x from both sides of 2x − 3 = x + 2 is valid for exactly the same reason subtracting 3 is: x names some number too, so it obeys arithmetic like any other term.

Where this shows up

An investigator cancels an identical unknown baseline from both groups of a study before comparing the two results — the same "subtract a term from both sides" move, applied to research data.

Misconception

What students think: letters cannot be subtracted from both sides, since a variable doesn't feel like a "real" quantity the way a number does.
Fix: point out that x names some number, so subtracting it is the same kind of move as subtracting 3.

Try this

Solve 3x = 2x + 18 by transposing the variable term first.

Slide 7

Clearing a fraction first

5x + 7/2 = 3/2x − 14 is easier once every term — fractional and whole alike — is multiplied by 2: 10x + 7 = 3x − 28. Clearing a fraction means operating on the entire side, not just the fractional terms. A technician converts every measurement to one common unit before combining them, for the same reason.

For (6x+1)/3 + 1 = (x−3)/6, multiply by 6 — the LCM of 3 and 6 — not by 18, their product. The smaller correct choice keeps the arithmetic lighter. Scheduling two chores that recur every three and every six days rests on the same lowest common multiple.

Misconception

What students think: only the terms carrying denominators need multiplying — clearing fractions is read as fixing the fractions, not operating on an entire side.
Fix: write the multiplication against every single term, whole numbers included.

Slide 8

Open the brackets, then check both sides

For 5x − 2(2x − 7) = 2(3x − 1) + 7/2, expand fully first: LHS = x + 14, RHS = 6x + 3/2. Require the fully expanded line written on its own before any transposing begins — interleaving the two moves is where sign errors creep in. A quantity surveyor expands every bracketed allowance in a quotation before totalling, for the same reason.

Solving x + 14 = 6x + 3/2 gives x = 5/2. Substitute back: LHS = 33/2, RHS = 33/2 — the check is a genuine verification, catching a sign slip the working itself might have missed. A quality inspector measures a finished part against the specification rather than trusting the machine setting.

Misconception 1

What students think: transposing can begin before the brackets are opened.
Fix: require the fully expanded equation written on a line of its own before any transposing starts.

Misconception 2

What students think: the final check can be skipped once the working feels confident.
Fix: mark a worked example where a sign slip is caught only by the final comparison of the two sides.

Slide 9

Where linear equations are used

Numbers, ages, perimeters, and combinations of currency notes are the chapter's own recurring applications — all solved by choosing a variable, writing an equation, and applying exactly the steps from this chapter. The choice of what to call x matters: a well-chosen unknown makes the equation shorter and the arithmetic lighter.

Where this shows up

Settling a shared expense among contributors who each hand over a different number of notes is solved this way — one equation, one chosen unknown, the rest follows.

Misconception

What students think: the variable must always stand for the exact quantity the question asks about.
Fix: solve one age problem twice, with two different choices of unknown, and compare how hard each version becomes.

Classroom activity

Pose a combination-of-notes problem: a total of ₹590 made of ₹50 and ₹10 notes, 19 notes in total. Have half the class let x be the number of ₹50 notes, and the other half let x be the number of ₹10 notes. Compare which choice solves faster.
