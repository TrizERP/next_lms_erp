Linear Equations in One Variable

5x = 25 is an equation; 5x alone is an expression. That one distinction — the equals sign — is the whole chapter's foundation. Everything else is a method for finding which value of the variable makes the two sides equal, then checking it. Open your lesson by sorting a mixed list of expressions and equations before naming either term.

Before you teach

Can you explain, in one sentence, why 2x − 3 cannot be "solved" the way 2x − 3 = 7 can?

Slide 2

Expression or equation?

5x, 2x − 3, 3x + y are expressions — no equals sign, nothing to solve. 5x = 25, 2x − 3 = 9 are equations: a claim that two expressions have the same value. Teach the sort before the vocabulary.

LHS is the expression left of the equals sign, RHS the expression right of it. Show an equation like 9 = 2x − 3 early — the bare number isn't always on the right.

x = 5 solves 2x − 3 = 7 because LHS = 2×5−3 = 7 = RHS. x = 10 does not: LHS = 17 ≠ RHS. Only certain values keep the two sides equal — those are the solutions.

Common misconception

Students expect the RHS to always hold the answer and the LHS to hold the working — arithmetic habit carries the result after the equals sign. Show equations like 9 = 2x − 3 where the bare number sits on the left instead, breaking that expectation early.

Slide 3

What makes an equation linear

2xy + 5 has two variables, x and y — it's excluded here. This chapter restricts to equations with exactly one variable.

2x, 3y − 7, 12 − 5z are linear: the highest power of the variable is 1. x² + 1 is not. "Linear" describes the exponent, never how many terms an expression has.

One variable plus degree 1 together define a linear equation in one variable — the type studied through this entire chapter, and the simple equations from earlier classes were always this type.

Common misconception

Students read the 5 in 12 − 5z as a power of z, since it sits right beside the letter, and conclude the expression isn't linear. Contrast 5z with z raised to the power five, showing exactly where an index is written.

Try this

Is 2x² + 1 = 9 a linear equation in one variable? Justify using both rules — one variable, and highest power.

Slide 4

The balance principle

Treat the two sides of an equation as a balance: perform the same operation on both sides, and the balance holds. x + 3 = 7, subtract 3 from both sides, and x + 3 − 3 = 7 − 3 gives x = 4 — the scale never tips.

Checking means substituting the answer back into the original equation — a different route than the one that found it, not a repeat of the same working.

Common misconception

Students operate on whichever side looks awkward and leave the other side untouched — tidying one side is a more visible goal than preserving equality. A real two-pan balance, with a weight lifted off only one pan, makes the resulting tilt immediate and obvious.

Slide 5

Transposing: the shortcut for balancing

Subtracting x from both sides amounts to moving x across the equals sign with its sign flipped — that shortcut is transposing. It is not a separate rule to memorise; it is the balancing step written faster.

Common misconception

Students recite that a term changes sign when it crosses the equals sign, without knowing why — the shortcut is taught as a rule to memorise before the balancing step it abbreviates has been shown. Write the full addition or subtraction on both sides once, then show the shortcut produces the identical line.

Try this

Transpose the 7 in 7x + 7 = −28 to the other side. What does the equation become?

Slide 6

A variable on each side

2x − 3 = x + 2 has expressions with a variable on both sides, not just a bare number on one. Comparing two phone tariffs, each charging per minute, naturally produces this shape of equation.

Subtracting x from both sides of 2x − 3 = x + 2 is valid for exactly the same reason subtracting 3 is: x names some number too, so it obeys arithmetic like any other term.

Common misconception

Students accept subtracting 3 from both sides but hesitate over subtracting x, treating the variable as an empty placeholder rather than a number. Point out that x names some number, so subtracting it is the same kind of move as subtracting 3.

Try this

Solve 3x = 2x + 18 by transposing the variable term first.

Slide 7

Clearing a fraction first

5x + 7/2 = 3/2x − 14 is easier once every term — fractional and whole alike — is multiplied by 2: 10x + 7 = 3x − 28. Clearing a fraction means operating on the entire side, not just the fractional terms.

For (6x+1)/3 + 1 = (x−3)/6, multiply by 6 — the LCM of 3 and 6 — not by 18, their product. The smaller correct choice keeps the arithmetic lighter.

Common misconception

Students multiply only the terms carrying denominators and leave whole-number terms untouched — clearing fractions is read as fixing the fractions rather than operating on an entire side. Write the multiplication against every single term, whole numbers included.

Slide 8

Open the brackets, then check both sides

For 5x − 2(2x − 7) = 2(3x − 1) + 7/2, expand fully first: LHS = x + 14, RHS = 6x + 3/2. Write the fully expanded line on its own before any transposing — interleaving the two is where sign errors creep in.

Solving gives x = 5/2. Substitute back: LHS = RHS = 33/2 — a genuine verification, catching a sign slip the working itself might have missed.

Numbers, ages, perimeters, and currency notes are the chapter's own recurring applications — all solved by choosing a variable and writing an equation.

Common misconception

Students always let the variable stand for the quantity a question asks about, even when another choice is far simpler — word-problem drills usually name the unknown for them, so choosing it is never actually practised. Solve one age problem twice with different choices of unknown and compare how hard each becomes.
